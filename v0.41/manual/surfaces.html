Surface areas ​

Before this page

ChemicalSystem and ChemicalState; the theory of site families is in Chemistry that happens on a surface.

A reaction on a solid needs to know how much of that solid the liquid can reach. This page is the syntax for saying so: the area models, what each one assumes, and why the measurement an area comes from is part of its type.

An area is a model, not a number ​

julia
using ChemistryLab, DynamicQuantities

BETSurfaceArea(90.0u"m^2/kg")
BETSurfaceArea{Float64}(90.0)

Every model answers one question — total_area, in m² — from the current molar amount, the initial one, and the molar mass:

julia
bet = BETSurfaceArea(90.0)           # a plain number is read as m²/kg
total_area(bet, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1)     # n = 0.01 mol, n₀ = 0.05 mol, M = 0.1 kg/mol
0.09000000000000001

The initial amount is in the signature even where it is unused, so that a model which does depend on it needs no change anywhere else. surface_area is the two-amount-free shorthand, total_area(model, n, n, M).

The four models ​

modelareawhat it assumes
FixedSurfaceAreaa total m², constantthe area is set externally and does not move over the run
BETSurfaceAreaA_spec × n × Mthe specific area is constant, so area follows the remaining mass
BlaineSurfaceAreasame law, Blaine finenessas above, for a binder characterized by air permeability
ShrinkingCoreAreaA₀ (n/n₀)^pgrains consumed from the outside, p = 2/3 for spheres

BETSurfaceArea and ShrinkingCoreArea with p = 1 are the same law, which is the point of writing the second as a family rather than a special case:

julia
linear = ShrinkingCoreArea(bet; exponent = 1)
total_area(linear, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1) ≈ total_area(bet, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1)
true

At p = 2/3 the area outlives the mass, because area goes as the square of a radius and amount as its cube:

julia
sphere = ShrinkingCoreArea(bet)                     # p = 2/3 by default
n₀ = 0.05
[(f, total_area(sphere, f * n₀, n₀, 0.1)) for f in (1.0, 0.5, 0.1)]
3-element Vector{Tuple{Float64, Float64}}:
 (1.0, 0.45)
 (0.5, 0.2834822352814057)
 (0.1, 0.09694955814294816)

At exhaustion the power law has an infinite slope, which an integrator does reach. The regularization is documented under SHRINK_FLOOR: the area is exactly zero at zero, exactly A₀ at n₀, and differentiable throughout.

What no model here does

None of them describes an area blocked by something growing on it. A hydrate layer covering a clinker grain reduces the accessible area without consuming the grain, so it depends on the whole assemblage rather than on one amount. That is a different object: a factor of the rate law, CoveringLayer with covered for a layer counted in the sites it blocks, and PoreHumidity for the water the grain sees.

A powder of several sizes ​

The fines of a ground mineral carry most of its surface, so a single shrinking core misplaces the surface over the run: the fines are consumed first, and with them the surface the coarse grains do not have. size_classes cuts the size distribution by volume into classes of equal volume, each a copy of the species with its own amount and shrinking-core area, ready for one rate law. The particles of class , between the volume fractions   and , have as spheres the specific surface

exact for a LogNormalPSD (a closed form in the normal distribution function) and for a measured TabulatedPSD, so that the classes add up to the surface of the whole powder.

julia
subs = Dict(symbol(s) => s for s in build_species(datapath("cemdata18-thermofun.json"); verbose = false))
classes = size_classes(subs["C3S"], LogNormalPSD(; d50 = 10u"μm", d90 = 30u"μm"), 4; mass = 1u"kg")
[(symbol(c.species), c.amount, c.diameter * 1e6, specific_area(c.surface.initial)) for c in classes]
4-element Vector{Tuple{String, Float64, Float64, Float64}}:
 ("C3S", 1.0949832252353977, 3.0239977970476106, 635.9597543088732)
 ("C3S_2", 1.0949832252353977, 7.467501118334013, 257.534731574305)
 ("C3S_3", 1.0949832252353977, 13.030744734953513, 147.58487984822253)
 ("C3S_4", 1.0949832252353977, 27.57088184737544, 69.75260735898597)

The columns are the species of each class, its amount in mol, its Sauter diameter in µm and its specific surface in m²/kg. One class is the mineral itself with the surface of the whole powder.

Two areas of different measurements do not compare ​

This is the part worth reading even if the rest looks obvious. A BET area and a Blaine fineness are different measurements of different things, and the package now refuses to mix them:

julia
# The fineness figures of Lavergne et al. (2018), data/literature/Lavergne2018.json
lav(q) = literature_value("Lavergne2018", q)
try
    area_ratio(BETSurfaceArea(lav("bet_silica_fume")), BlaineSurfaceArea(lav("blaine_ref_clinker")))
catch err
    showerror(stdout, err)
end
ArgumentError: cannot compare a BET area with a Blaine area: they measure different things, and their ratio is not a fineness factor. Convert deliberately, or use two models of the same kind.

The first number in that example is silica fume's BET area, about 20 000 m²/kg, against the Blaine fineness of reference of the clinker rate laws (Lavergne et al., 2018). The same article takes 2 000 m²/kg as the effective Blaine fineness of silica fume, which its kinetics were fitted with. Using the first where the second belongs multiplies its hydration rate by ten, and before this refusal nothing stopped it.

Same measurement, and the ratio is just a ratio:

julia
filler = lav("blaine_limestone_filler")   # a limestone filler, 462 m²/kg
area_ratio(BlaineSurfaceArea(filler), BlaineSurfaceArea(lav("blaine_ref_clinker")))
1.2

which is exactly what blaine_factor computes. A bare number handed to it is still read as a Blaine fineness — that is the historical contract and it has not changed — so type the argument when you want the check:

julia
blaine_factor(filler), blaine_factor(BlaineSurfaceArea(filler))
(1.2, 1.2)

A surface names its support ​

SurfaceSupport ties an area model to the solid carrying it:

julia
SurfaceSupport("calcite", "Cal", BETSurfaceArea(90.0))
SurfaceSupport("calcite", on Cal, BETSurfaceArea)

The rate factories take one in place of a bare area model, and that is what lets the molar mass be looked up once from the named species. It matters: a species with no :M used to fall back to 0.1 kg/mol silently, which scales the reactive area and therefore the whole rate. It now raises, naming the species.

For a sorbent whose amount is prescribed rather than solved for, leave the host out:

julia
SurfaceSupport("inert sorbent", FixedSurfaceArea(0.5u"m^2"))
SurfaceSupport("inert sorbent", prescribed support, FixedSurfaceArea)

See also ​

  • Rate laws, and every parameter in them — where the reactive area enters a rate law, and why the fineness correction of the empirical cement laws is a constant rather than an area.

  • Surfaces API — the docstrings.

Site families: a conserved quantity that is not an element ​

A surface that only has an area does nothing. What makes it chemistry is a finite number of sites, and species that occupy them.

The package already had a conserved quantity that is not a chemical element: electric charge, carried as the pseudo-element :Zz in a formula and turned into a conservation row by the ordinary matrix assembly. A site family works the same way, with a pseudo-element of its own from SITE_SYMBOLS:

julia
using ChemistryLab, DynamicQuantities

surf(sym) = Species(sym; aggregate_state = AS_SURFACE, class = SC_SURFCOMPLEX)

free = surf("XsOH")          # the free site — a species like any other
prot = surf("XsOH2+")        # protonated
depr = surf("XsO-")          # deprotonated
atoms(free)
OrderedCollections.OrderedDict{Symbol, Int64} with 3 entries:
  :Xs => 1
  :O  => 1
  :H  => 1

:Xs is the family; O and H are the real atoms. That matters for the mass:

julia
free[:M]                     # oxygen and hydrogen only
0.017006999994122744 kg mol⁻¹

The site weighs nothing, by declaration. The mass of the support is carried once, by its own mineral species; adding it again on every occupied site would be double counting. The same applies to volume — a surface complex has no standard molar volume, because the volume it occupies is the host's.

Declaring the family ​

julia
support = SurfaceSupport("hydrous ferric oxide", nothing, FixedSurfaceArea(600.0u"m^2"))
family = SiteFamily("Hfo_s", free, [prot, depr];
                    capacity = TotalSiteAmount(5.0e-6u"mol"), support)
SiteFamily("Hfo_s", :Xs, 3 members, TotalSiteAmount on hydrous ferric oxide)

The capacity comes in three shapes because the published data does, and converting between them needs a quantity that is usually not measured: TotalSiteAmount for a prescribed sorbent, AreaSiteDensity in mol/m² for the oxide literature, MassSiteDensity in mol/kg for a clay exchange capacity — the last needing no area at all.

julia
site_moles(family, 0.0, 0.0, 0.0)     # this one ignores the host entirely
5.0e-6

The site balance falls out of the matrix ​

Build the system with the free site species as the primary, and the conservation row appears on its own:

julia
aq(sym, cl = SC_AQSOLUTE) = Species(sym; aggregate_state = AS_AQUEOUS, class = cl)
species = [aq("H2O@", SC_AQSOLVENT), aq("H+"), aq("OH-"), aq("Ca+2"), free, prot, depr]
cs = ChemicalSystem(species, [species[1], species[2], species[4], free];
                    site_families = [family])
row = cs.SM.A[findfirst(p -> symbol(p) == "XsOH", cs.SM.primaries), :]
Int.(row)
7-element Vector{Int64}:
 0
 0
 0
 0
 1
 1
 1

One per surface species, zero on every aqueous one: that row is n_free + n_protonated + n_deprotonated = N_t. And the rest of the matrix is the protolysis, XsOH2+ = XsOH + H+ and XsO- = XsOH − H+, written by the same assembly that writes every other reaction.

The primary is the free site

Declaring a neutral Species("Xs") as the site primary looks equivalent and is not. It is inconsistent in charge with a neutral free site, which flips the exact rank test inside StoichMatrix and adds a spurious charge component — silently. A support whose sites follow their host accepts the free site as primary too, or a bare component carrying the free site's charge (see below).

What is refused, and why ​

Each of these produces a wrong number rather than an error if it is allowed through, so each raises at construction: a member carrying no site symbol or two of them; a free site occupying anything but one site; a species in two families; two families sharing one pseudo-element; and an AS_SURFACE species belonging to no declared family at all — it would carry a pseudo-element into the matrix, and so a row, with nothing mixing on it.

Multidentate species are refused too, for now. The site balance holds for any denticity — Xs2OCa contributes two — but ideal mixing of occupied and free sites is exact only for one, and the quasi-chemical treatment of the rest is a separate model this release does not provide. Saying so is better than returning a number that looks like an isotherm.

Where the species end up ​

AS_SURFACE is its own aggregate state, deliberately. Surface complexes are not in crystal(cs), because idx_crystal is what the solver and the start repair read as "a pure mineral phase", which a site occupancy is not:

julia
symbol.(surface(cs))
3-element Vector{String}:
 "XsOH"
 "XsOH2+"
 "XsO-"

They are nonetheless counted in the solid compartment of a state, because that is where their matter is.

What mixes on the sites, and what Langmuir has to do with it ​

Declaring the family is half of it. The other half is the activity of its members, and it is the simplest one there is: a site fraction.

julia
grp = cs.site_groups[1]              # the family's members, free site first
n = zeros(length(cs.species))
n[1] = 55.5                          # water
n[grp] .= [5.0e-4, 3.0e-4, 2.0e-4]   # free, protonated, deprotonated
lna = activity_model(cs, DiluteSolutionModel())(n, (ϵ = 1.0e-30, T = 298.15))
[exp(lna[i]) for i in grp]           # each one is n_i / Σ n
3-element Vector{Float64}:
 0.5
 0.3
 0.2

Half the sites free, three tenths protonated, two tenths deprotonated — the composition put in, read back as activities.

That is IdealSiteMixing, and every activity model the package ships applies it — dilute, HKF (Helgeson et al., 1981), Davies (Davies, 1962) and Pitzer (Pitzer, 1975) alike — because a site fraction owes nothing to the ionic strength of the solution beside it.

Langmuir is the consequence, not the premise ​

No isotherm is written anywhere in this package. Put the site balance n_free + Σ n_j = N next to the mass-action law of each binding reaction, with the free site's activity in it, and eliminate the free site:

— competitive Langmuir, with the shared denominator that is the signature of a finite capacity. test/surface_complexation.jl checks it against that derived form on an amphoteric surface across four pH values, to a relative 1e-6.

Do not apply a surface activity coefficient on top of this

The literature's surface activity coefficient, γ_L = 1/(1 − θ), exists to make an eliminated free site reproduce what an explicit one already does. This formulation keeps the free site as a species, so the correction is already in the mixing. Applying it again counts saturation twice.

The identity is worth knowing as a diagnostic, and the test suite asserts it rather than assuming it: at equilibrium 1/(1 − θ) equals N/n_free to 1e-8.

In the solver, a family is a phase ​

A site family reaches the dual solver as a mixing phase, like the aqueous solution and like a solid solution — never as a set of pure phases, which is what it would be if it kept its default classification. Two settings differ from a solid solution's, and neither is cosmetic:

  • the reference member is the free site, not the most abundant one. It is the state a fresh surface is mostly in, and a family cannot run out of it without the occupied states taking its place;

  • the phase is always present. A family's total is pinned by a conservation row, so a presence test on the size of its members would refuse to activate it from a cold start and leave Σ n = N > 0 unsatisfiable from the first Newton iteration.

Cation exchange: declaring the convention ​

A permanent-charge exchanger uses the same machinery with the sites counted as units of charge. Write the divalent form with two of the family's pseudo-elements and the conservation row becomes the cation exchange capacity:

julia
using ChemistryLab, DynamicQuantities

ex(sym) = Species(sym; aggregate_state = AS_SURFACE, class = SC_SURFCOMPLEX)
aq(sym, cl = SC_AQSOLUTE) = Species(sym; aggregate_state = AS_AQUEOUS, class = cl)

naX, kX, caX = ex("NaXc"), ex("KXc"), ex("CaXc2")
atoms(caX)          # two units of charge, from the formula
OrderedCollections.OrderedDict{Symbol, Int64} with 2 entries:
  :Ca => 1
  :Xc => 2

The convention is part of the declaration. There is no default, and nothing is converted behind your back:

julia
support = SurfaceSupport("clay", nothing, FixedSurfaceArea(1.0))
family = SiteFamily("X", naX, [kX, caX];
                    capacity = TotalSiteAmount(1.0e-3u"mol"),   # moles of charge

One for each monovalent form, two for the calcium: the row counts equivalents, and it counts them because the formula said so.

The two conventions are not a rescaling of each other

VanselowMixing takes the activity to be a mole fraction, GainesThomasMixing an equivalent fraction. On a homovalent exchange they are the same number. On a heterovalent one they are not, and the conversion between the constants fitted under each depends on the exchanger's composition — which is what you are solving for.

Factors of 2, 3 or 4 appear in the literature; they are trace-composition limits. A constant fitted under one convention and used under the other is a different model.

There is no free site on an exchanger — every charge is compensated — so the first member is a reference form rather than a vacancy. reference_member says so where free_site would mislead.

Multidentate occupancy is refused under IdealSiteMixing and accepted under either exchange convention. That is not an inconsistency: on an exchanger the sites counted are charges, and a divalent cation neutralizes two of them without straddling two surface groups. A surface complex that genuinely straddles two sites is a combinatorial problem this release does not solve.

A charged surface of three planes ​

ChargePlanes describes a surface whose charge sits in up to three planes: the surface, the head of the Stern layer and the start of the diffuse layer (Chemistry that happens on a surface §11). It is declared with the area, in m², and the capacitances between the planes, in F/m²; C2 = Inf, the default, merges the outer two, which is the basic Stern model:

julia
using ChemistryLab

stern = ChargePlanes(; area = 105.0, C1 = 0.9)
triple = ChargePlanes(; area = 105.0, C1 = 1.4, C2 = 0.2)
(stern.C2, triple.ε_r)
(Inf, 78.24514581696772)

Each member of a family states the charge it puts on each plane with with_plane_charges; a member that does not puts its formal charge on plane 0. On goethite a singly coordinated group carries −1/2, which its free form puts on plane 0, and an ion pair of chloride with the protonated group puts +1/2 there and −1 on plane 2, where the basic Stern model places the ion pairs:

julia
sp(sym) = Species(sym; aggregate_state = AS_SURFACE, class = SC_SURFCOMPLEX)
free = with_plane_charges(sp("XsOH"), -0.5)
prot = with_plane_charges(sp("XsOH2+"), 0.5)
pair = with_plane_charges(sp("XsOH2Cl"), 0.5, 0, -1)

The formula keeps whole charges, for the conservation of charge. What the planes carry may differ from it by the charge of the group, the same for every member of a family; SiteFamily refuses a member that differs by another amount, since a reaction between members would then move charge in one count and not in the other.

The three potentials, in units of , of a surface carrying given amounts of members in a solution of given ionic strength:

julia
c = [(-0.5, 0.0, 0.0), (0.5, 0.0, 0.0), (0.5, 0.0, -1.0)]   # free, protonated, pair
n = [2.0e-4, 3.4e-4, 4.0e-5]                                 # mol
ψ = charge_planes_potentials(stern, c, n, 0.01, 298.15)
ψ .* (R_GAS * 298.15 / FARADAY)                              # in volts
(0.1985183578497753, 0.10662756535138483, 0.10662756535138483)

The families of one surface share its planes: they name the same support and must declare the same area, capacitances and permittivity. A solve carries the three potentials as unknowns, and its certificate reports a self-consistent speciation, the diffuse layer not being the gradient of an energy.

When the support dissolves or precipitates ​

Everything above poses the site budget once. A sorbent that dissolves, or one that precipitates during a calculation, carries its sites with it, so the budget has to follow its amount instead. Ask for it on the support, and give the family a capacity measured per unit mass or per unit specific area, the two that are proportional to how much host there is:

julia
support = SurfaceSupport(
    "hydrous ferric oxide", "Fe(OH)3(am)", FixedSurfaceArea(1.0);
    coupling = SITES_FOLLOW_HOST,
)
family = SiteFamily("Xw", free, [prot, depr]; capacity = MassSiteDensity(ν / M), support)

Naming a host does not couple anything on its own, since the kinetics names one to find the amount a rate law scales with; the coupling is asked for.

ν, the moles of sites one mole of host carries, is Γ·a·M for an area density and q·M for a mass density, and sites_per_host returns it. Which capacities qualify is measured rather than listed: the capacity is evaluated at two scaled host amounts and the budget has to scale with them. A TotalSiteAmount and an area density over a FixedSurfaceArea are constants and are refused; a ShrinkingCoreArea passes at p = 1 and nowhere else.

The free sites are part of the host ​

The host's database formula already contains the surface groups its sites are made of: Fe(OH)₃ holds the hydroxyls its surface exposes, and the database energy of the solid is that of the whole grain. The package therefore counts the free sites as part of the host, removing ν free sites' worth of atoms from the host's formula in conservation_matrix, so that every atom is counted once and the charge stays conserved while the host dissolves or grows. Two consequences follow for the declaration.

The free site sits at zero energy, and every other member is written relative to it through its reaction constant, which is what site_family does. With a fixed budget any value would cancel, both sides of every surface reaction carrying one site; with a budget that follows its host the value enters the host's own energy. host_coupling_bias reads, in log units of the host's solubility, how far the declaration is from zero, and a family above 0.05 is refused with the value to use. Giving the free site the energy of the matter it carries, μ°(H₂O) − μ°(H⁺) = −237.2 kJ/mol for XwOH, would count that energy twice: at the weak-site density of Dzombak and Morel (1990), ν = 0.2, it is worth 8.3 log units.

The free site is neutral, and the host's formula contains ν free sites' worth of every element; either failing is refused by name. The primary of the site row may be the free site itself, as above, or a bare site component, Species("Xw+"), which is a component and need not be among the species; the two give the same answer.

A C-S-H described by CSHQ is not yet a host

Sites that bind calcium or alkalis cannot be hosted on the CSHQ solid solution: its energies were fitted on the total calcium of the gel, surface included, so the calcium on the surface would be counted twice. The construction of the system refuses it. Chloride binding in blended cements describes the two ways around it that exist today.

A host that a rate law dissolves or grows ​

The host may be a kinetic species of a KineticsProblem. Its amount is then a variable of the integration, known before every solve of the equilibrium partition, so the budget is not solved for: it is N(n_host) = site_moles(family, n_host, n₀, M), whatever the capacity, and the run moves it in the site row of the partition's budget,

Over a ShrinkingCoreArea the sites follow the area of grains dissolving from the outside, , which a host at equilibrium cannot do. A vanishing site leaves the partition as a free site, so the free site must be bare, carrying no atom and no charge: what a complex held then returns to the solution. Write the free site as Xs and every state relative to it, Xs + H₂O = XsOH⁻ + H⁺. The implicit step (KineticStepSolver) poses the host and its sites in one conservation row, so it takes only a budget linear in the host.

What to check afterwards ​

site_budget_residual reports, per family, the moles of sites the state carries minus the moles its capacity declares. Zero means the two say the same thing. host_consistent_state derives the free-site amount from the declaration so they do, and check_site_budget turns a disagreement into an error instead of a silently different calculation.
