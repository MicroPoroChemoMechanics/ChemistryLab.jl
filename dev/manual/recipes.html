Recipes: materials, extents and what has not reacted ​

Before this page

The binders, and what distinguishes them and Solving an equilibrium.

A cement calculation starts from materials (a clinker, a slag, a limestone, water), their masses, and how much of each has reacted at the age of interest. What has reacted enters the equilibrium; what has not stays in the paste as a solid that takes up volume and holds matter, without taking part in the chemistry. Written by hand, this bookkeeping is repeated on every page and each copy can drift from the others. This page describes the layer that does it once: Material, Recipe, budget and the processes built on them.

Materials ​

A material is a list of constituents, each with its mass fraction of the material and its own degree of reaction:

  • a MineralConstituent is a database phase (alite, calcite, quartz);

  • an OxideConstituent is known by its oxide analysis only, like the glass of a slag, and enters the equilibrium through its elements.

Published materials are built from data/recipe_templates.toml, which names the literature file each number is read from. The system below is a small paste: the clinker phases, the usual hydrates and CSHQ.

julia
using ChemistryLab, OptimaSolver, DynamicQuantities
substances = build_species(datapath("cemdata18-thermofun.json"); verbose = false)
db = Dict(symbol(s) => s for s in substances)
pure = split("C3S C2S C3A C4AF Gp Anh Cal Portlandite ettringite monosulphate12 " *
             "monocarbonate hemicarbonate C4AH13 C3AH6 C3FH6 straetlingite " *
             "hydrotalcite Mg2AlC0.5OH Brc FeOOHmic AlOHmic Amor-Sl K2SO4")
csh = ["CSHQ-JenD", "CSHQ-JenH", "CSHQ-TobD", "CSHQ-TobH", "KSiOH", "NaSiOH"]
species = speciation(substances, vcat(pure, csh); aggregate_state = [AS_AQUEOUS],
                     exclude_species = split("H2@ O2@ CH4@"))
cs = ChemicalSystem(species, CEMDATA_PRIMARIES;
                    solid_solutions = [SolidSolutionPhase("CSHQ", [db[m] for m in csh])])

How much has reacted ​

An extent is a number between 0 and 1, a function of time, or one of ConstantExtent, TabulatedExtent (measured degrees of reaction, interpolated), LogisticExtent, ParrottKillohExtent (the rate law of Parrott and Killoh (1984) integrated) and CappedExtent (any of them under Powers' water limit (Powers, 1948)). with_extents sets them by constituent, and material_extent sets one for the material as a whole; the effective extent of a constituent is the product of the two (effective_extent). A glass described by its oxides alone has one extent, the material's.

julia

A recipe, and what it puts into the equilibrium ​

A Recipe gives the binder's mass fractions and the water/binder ratio. Every amount is for 100 g of binder unless binder_mass says otherwise, so a mass read off the answer is in grams per 100 g of binder.

recipe = Recipe(pc => 0.7, slag => 0.3; w_b = 0.45)
bud = budget(recipe, cs)

The rules budget applies:

whatwhere it goes
the reacted part of a mineral constituentthe equilibrium, as that species
the reacted part of an oxide constituentthe equilibrium, as its elements (oxide_budget)
water, saltsthe equilibrium, always completely
the unreacted part of a constituentthe residue: its mass, its volume, its enthalpy
an oxide whose element the system has no primary forthe residue, with reason = :not_in_system

The residue keeps its volume from the molar volume of its species or from the density of an oxide constituent, and its enthalpy from the enthalpy of formation. A glass has no formula, so no database gives either: unless its source does, they are nothing, and a volume or a heat that needs them is reported missing (NaN), never estimated.

Notes

  • alpha0 and alpha1 are the dimensionless Guggenheim interaction parameters, a0 = alpha0 R T and a1 = alpha1 R T in the Redlich-Kister form. The gap columns are the miscibility gap the parameters reproduce, as the article prints it: X(SO4) in the solid for the AFt pair, X(OH) for the AFm pair. The sign of alpha1 depends on which end-member comes first; with C4AH13 first, as in the miscibility_gap example, the spinodal the parameters give, X(OH) from 0.63 to 0.91, lies inside the printed gap.
  • For the AFt pair the article notes that the parameters are downscaled to a 1 CO2 : 1 SO3 replacement, instead of the 3 CO2 : 3 SO3 of its refs. [4, 7].
  • solubility_products: 'phase' is the package's symbol for the solid and is empty for the two rows of Table 2 the vendored database cannot be checked on, nitrite-AFm (the solid ships as mononitrite, the aqueous NO2- does not) and Fe-Friedel's salt (absent); their dissolution products are under the symbols mononitrite and C4FCl2H10, and their Table 1 properties in solid_standard_properties. 'printed' is the table's own name, without its footnote marks except in the Table 3 rows, where they tell two M4AH10 apart: * marks the tentative value recommended for Portland cement systems (the package's hydrotalcite), ** the tentative values recommended for alkali-activated materials (the MgAl-OH-LDH ideal solid solution).
  • solid_standard_properties: the two Table 1 rows the shipped database is not checked on through Table 2. Both carry the four heat-capacity coefficients as printed. Fe-Friedel's salt carries note k (an ideal solid solution, here with Friedel's salt C4ACl2H10, which carries it too) and note l on its enthalpy and volume: 'typing error in [37], recalculated from Gf and S from [37]. Volume calculated from XRD data [37]', [37] being the thesis of B. Z. Dilnesa (EPFL, 2012). Its a2, printed -1.61x10^4 where the other AFm phases carry a few 10^6, is transcribed as printed; at 25 C it weighs 0.2 J/(mol K) in Cp. Its dfH is consistent with dfG and S at 298.15 K: with the entropies of the elements the Cemdata18 file carries, dfH - T (S - sum of the elements' S) - dfG = -0.02 kJ/mol, and dfG with Table 2: through the Cemdata18 aqueous Gibbs energies, log Ks0 = -28.61 against -28.62 printed. The nitrite-AFm row gives the same log Ks0 to 0.001 through the NO2- of the slop98 database.
  • dissolution_products: Table 2 writes its reactions over Al(OH)4-, Fe(OH)4- and SiO(OH)3- (H3SiO4- for thaumasite); the CEMDATA18 primaries AlO2-, FeO2- and HSiO3- differ from those by 2, 2 and 1 H2O. The coefficients are the table's, the species are the primaries, and the water is left to the hydrogen balance.
  • aqueous_species: Table D.1 heads its volume column V0 (J/bar), but the values are in cm3/mol (Ca+2 at -18.44; -18.44 J/bar would be -184.4 cm3/mol). The HKF columns are the printed numbers of the scaled columns, in the calorimetric units of the HKF papers: a1_e1 holds a1 x 10, a2_em2 a2 x 10^-2, a4_em4 a4 x 10^-4, c2_em4 c2 x 10^-4 and omega_em5 omega x 10^-5.
  • gases: here the volume column is in J/bar (2479 J/bar = 24.79 L/mol, the ideal-gas molar volume at 298.15 K and 1 bar).

porosity is referred to the initial volume, that of the reactants, the water and the residue:

the void being the chemical shrinkage the reactions did not refill. With a slag glass of unknown density, the residue's volume is missing and the porosity cannot be computed: NaN, which is the honest answer. volume_fractions of a recipe state divides the same initial volume among the phases, the unreacted parts and the void, and is refused for the same reason. bound_water is per gram of binder and pore_solution gives the pH and the dissolved elements, in mol per kg of water.

Bound water and heat ​

bound_water is the water the solids would lose on ignition (ignition_loss), with that of an unreacted mineral such as gypsum, over the binder mass. It is the whole of what a thermogram integrates to. A thermogravimetric reading taken between 105 °C and 550 °C, a common choice, leaves part of it out, so the two are compared only over the same temperature window: bound_water(rs; window = (T₁, T₂), windows) counts what the solids release between the two temperatures, from a DecompositionWindow for each solid that holds water, and refuses a solid without one. The thermogram page shows where such windows come from.

At constant temperature and pressure, the heat a paste releases between two states is the fall of its enthalpy:

heat_release, given two states of one paste, computes it. The residue counts by what changed. The unreacted part of a clinker phase has the enthalpy of formation of its database record, so its change is counted. An oxide the system cannot hold, such as the titanium of this cement, keeps the same mass in both states and adds nothing, although no enthalpy is known for it. Where the unreacted part of a constituent without a sourced enthalpy changes, the heat is NaN rather than a number that leaves it out. The slag glass of this recipe is such a constituent, and enthalpy of the whole paste is NaN for the same reason.

A glass can be given an enthalpy of formation with glass_enthalpy, built from measurements on silicate glasses and stating what it assumes, which The enthalpy of a glass sets out:

julia
g = glass_enthalpy(oxides; ignore = ("P2O5",))     # an oxide with no crystal, by name
OxideConstituent("glass", oxides; enthalpy = g.enthalpy, source = "glass_enthalpy")

g.span and g.uncertainty say how far that enthalpy can be trusted, and g.unassigned how much of the glass the measured glasses could not take.

Below, the cement alone at two sets of extents, both chosen for the illustration, gives the heat released from the first to the second, in joules per gram of cement:

julia
early = with_extents(pc, Dict("C3S" => 0.40, "C2S" => 0.10, "C3A" => 0.50, "C4AF" => 0.30))
rs1, _ = equilibrate_certified(Recipe(early => 1.0; w_b = 0.45), cs; model)
rs2, _ = equilibrate_certified(Recipe(pc => 1.0; w_b = 0.45), cs; model)
(slag_paste = enthalpy(rs), heat = heat_release(rs1, rs2) / 100)
(slag_paste = NaN, heat = 177.71677814571478)

Processes ​

A process is a sequence of equilibria, each started from the previous answer, returned as a ProcessResult that process_table turns into a table:

  • hydrate: the recipe at several ages, its extents read at each;

  • blend: part of the binder replaced by a material, at several fractions;

  • titrate, carbonate, add_salt: a species added to the budget in steps;

  • leach: the pore solution replaced by water, step after step.

julia
co = carbonate(rs, [0.0, 0.2, 0.5])
process_table(co; phases = ["Portlandite", "Cal"])

From a recipe to a kinetic run ​

hydrate imposes the extents. Rate laws can decide them instead: a KineticsProblem built from the recipe takes each constituent named in rates whole and unreacted, as a kinetic species that dissolves into the primaries of the system, and every other constituent as the recipe has it at the start of the run. The same recipe then drives both.

julia
rates = Dict(
    "C3S" => parrott_killoh_avrami(PK84_PARAMS_C3S, "C3S"),
    "C2S" => parrott_killoh_avrami(PK84_PARAMS_C2S, "C2S"),
)
kp = KineticsProblem(Recipe(pc => 1.0; w_b = 0.45), cs, rates, (0.0, 28 * 86400.0))
for kr in kp.kinetic_reactions
    println(kr.reaction)
end
(CaO)₃SiO₂ = 3H₂O@ + SiO₂@ + 3Ca²⁺ + (-6)H⁺
(CaO)₂SiO₂ = 2H₂O@ + SiO₂@ + 2Ca²⁺ + (-4)H⁺

The dissolutions are those the conservation matrix of the system gives, so they conserve every element by construction. The reacted part of a constituent known by its oxides (the alkalis and the minor oxides of a clinker) enters as the primaries that carry its elements; its heat of dissolution is not part of the heat of the run. A glass has no formula to dissolve: glass_species gives it one from its own oxides, and with_species puts that species in its place, after which it can be given a rate (What a recipe puts into the equilibrium, Section 6). with_glass_species does both for every glass a dictionary of rates names, and adds the species to the system, with an enthalpy of formation estimated from the oxides so that the heat of the glass counts in a calorimeter. integrate then runs the problem, with an equilibrium solver for the partial equilibrium of the products, as in the kinetics tutorial.
