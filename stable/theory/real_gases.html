3. Which root ​

Below the critical temperature and between the spinodals the cubic has three real roots above : the vapor, the liquid and, between them, a root that describes no stable state. The stable phase is the root of least Gibbs energy, and that is the one taken. Where the two energies are equal, the fugacities of the vapor and the liquid are, which is the saturation: for carbon dioxide at 25 °C, with the constants of phreeqc.dat (Parkhurst and Appelo, 2013), the equation places it at 64.4 bar, against 64.35 bar for the reference equation of pure carbon dioxide that Spycher et al. (2003) quote. Across it, the root jumps from the vapor to the liquid and the fugacity stays continuous.

The root is found on plain numbers, in closed form, then lifted into the dual numbers of whatever is being differentiated by two Newton steps on the cubic  . The value does not move, and the derivatives come out as the implicit-function theorem gives them,   , exact to second order. At the critical point itself vanishes and the derivatives are infinite, which is the physics: the compressibility of a fluid diverges there.

5. What the equilibrium sees ​

The activity of a gas becomes

  • A gas alone is a pure phase whose activity,  , does not depend on its amount: its Gibbs energy is linear in that amount whatever the equation of state, the choice of root having already settled whether it is a vapor or a liquid. The problem keeps the convexity it had, and a certificate can still prove a global minimum (Proving that an answer is the answer).

  • A mixture of real gases can split into two fluids of different compositions, a liquid rich in one gas and a vapor rich in the other. Its energy is not convex in the composition, and a certificate proves no more than a local minimum.

A constraint that counts the volume of the system as , linear in the amounts, has no place for a phase whose molar volume depends on its composition, and refuses a real gas.
